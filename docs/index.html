One Million Americans Per Tile

The contiguous United States cut into 329 pieces that each hold about the same number of people. A tile's size is inverse population density — a million people fit in a few city blocks in one place, and sprawl across half a state in another.

How it's built

  1. Units. Census tract centers of population (~4,000 people each) — the practical finest nationwide unit.
  2. Bucketing. Grow contiguous clusters to a target headcount, then move border tracts to equalise population and gently round the shapes in.
  3. Colour. The fewest colours so no two neighbouring tiles match — the four-colour theorem in action. Colour means nothing.
  4. Render. Softly beveled tiles; rivers and lakes in blue.

How accurate can "equal" be?

Each tile aims for a target headcount; the band shows where they actually land. It tightens as the target grows — each tile then averages over more tracts — until it hits the floor set by the ~4,000-person tract atom. Nationally, anything from ~150k people per tile up is near-exact; only small targets (a tile is just a few tracts) are loose.

One way to draw them, or many?

Cluster the same target from different random seeds and measure how much they agree. With many small tiles the equal-population + contiguity constraints pin a near-unique answer; with few big tiles real freedom opens up and different seeds find different, equally-valid bucketings. So "how many ways are there?" depends on how big you make the pieces.

Why does the seed change the map? The tiles are grown one at a time from randomly-ordered starting points, and the population-equalising tweaks are then applied in random order too — so the seed decides where the buckets start and how ties are broken. Because there's no unique equal-population partition (many are equally valid), different seeds settle on different ones. With many small tiles the constraints are so tight that nearly every seed lands on almost the same answer; with a few big tiles, there's real room to differ — which is exactly what the agreement curve above measures.

(The colour is purely an aesthetic choice — it carries no data, and there are a dozen built-in palettes to pick from.)

Prior art

Partitioning a place into equal-population pieces is an old idea. The closest relatives are Neil Freeman's Fifty States with Equal Population, the Engaging Data / FlowingData "split the US by population" interactives, Slate's Equal Population Mapper, and capacity-constrained Voronoi / automated-redistricting methods generally. What's different here is the granularity — hundreds of tiles, so it reads as a texture rather than a few regions — and the art-first execution.
